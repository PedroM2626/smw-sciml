### 10.60 The Dashboard at the Top of This File, and the Two Instruments It Needed

The repository had telemetry plots and a schematic GIF, and neither could be looked at while the console ran. The hero above is the fix: one episode, the emulator's own RGB picture filling the left half and, down the right, the tile window the level-conditioned models read, what the actor thinks as eight named actions, the buttons the console actually received drawn on a controller, and the critic's $V(s)$; along the bottom, the twelve numbers the actor really is given and the stage progress bar. `scripts/record_dashboard_run.py` records it, `src/evaluation/dashboard_video.py` renders it (4.28 MB MP4, 3.91 MB GIF), and every number in this section is read out of the artifacts those two write.

**The grid now shares one coordinate system.** The first picture flipped the tile array, and the first 20x14 crop then put Mario's marker in upper-origin row 5 while claiming row 8. The renderer now uses the reference grid's exact hitbox cells - Mario at `((x + 8) >> 4, (y + 16) >> 4)`, each sprite at `((sprite_x + 8) >> 4, (sprite_y + 8) >> 4)` - and converts row 8 back through the upper-origin axis before drawing the marker. The five-colour display intentionally collapses decor and cells outside the buffer into one empty class (91.07% of this episode's displayed cells), while the recording retains every raw 16-bit id so that visual simplification cannot change the measured map.

**Which controller flies it.** Two learned checkpoints claim to be the repository's controller, so both were probed on the verified level-entry savestate for the same 600-frame horizon, and the rule was written down before the run: the arm that reaches the largest forward progress is the better of the two. That comparison is reported below either way - but the episode the README opens with is a different question, and 10.68 changed its answer: the hero shows a level being completed, so it is flown by the one controller this repository has that finishes the stage - the 10.66 seed-7 clearance draw, run until the engine itself ends it and kept recording through the exit sequence.

| arm | published overrides | frames | progress px | most frequent action | reflex frames |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `ppo` | yes | 600 | -7.5625 | `RUN_LEFT` x600 | 0 |
| `dagger` | yes | 600 | 1032.5000 | `Y/RIGHT` x267 | 13 |
| `dagger` | no | 600 | 1102.3750 | `Y/RIGHT` x277 | 0 |

The PPO primitive actor answers `RUN_LEFT` on all 600 frames and ends 7.5625 px behind where it started, at a mean top-1 probability of 0.1957 - a distribution that has not decided anything. The distilled DAgger actor traverses 1032.5000 px of the level with a mean top-1 button probability of 0.9068, so it stays the better of the two learned arms - and neither is the featured episode, because neither finishes the stage within any horizon this repository has flown it. The third row is the same actor with the published reflex vaults and jump pulse removed: it reaches 1102.3750 px, +69.8750 px further than the arm that keeps them, which is the cost of the override layer and the reason the dashboard shows the buttons the console actually received rather than the ones the actor proposed.

**The episode the video shows.** 6706 frames, 5010.0000 px of forward progress, ending as `engine_left_0x14_to_0x0C`, with the mode byte reading 0x0C, 0x0D, 0x0E, 0x14 - `0x14` for every frame the player was live, then the exit sequence's own march once the engine delivered its verdict, which the outro keeps recording. The camera travels 65519 px, Mario's screen position stays inside [-60494.31, 5024.69] px of the 256 px window, and the critic's prediction moves from 201.39 px to 70.94 px of discounted future progress over the episode.

**The pause, at last identified.** 10.59 found whole player records repeating on a console whose WRAM was still changing and called it a paused simulation without saying what paused it. With a picture attached to the recording, it says so: the engine opened its own message box, which reads *To do a spin jump, press the A Button*, and the box waits for A.

A is not one of the six channels in `ACTION_BUTTONS`, so no policy in this repository can dismiss the box it just walked into - the action space has no answer to the level's instruction. The recorder therefore lets an operator press it, holding the frozen player for 120 extra frames first so the text finishes printing, and marks every one of those 157 frames in the recording and on the dashboard. The frame the video holds on is cut out of the committed recording as `results/figures/dashboard_message_box.png` (frame 535, 975.5000 px into the level). The episode itself ends the way the featured controller's episodes now end: the mode byte leaves `0x14` for the engine's own exit sequence, and the outro keeps recording through it - the mode march and the map settling - so the video shows the level being completed rather than freezing on the flipped byte. The residue is no longer drawn as a panel; it is in the recording, and it satisfies the per-row identity exactly - `residue = 16 (x_(i+1) - x_i) - vx_i` - on every frame the player was live. The frames where the reading is not zero are the frames the engine itself did not move the position at the velocity's rate: 73.75% of the live frames move exactly as §4.1 says, and the exceptions are the holds and the goal tape's half-rate walk - which read exactly minus the velocity, because the position stood still while the speed byte did not - plus, at the clearance draw's run speed, the whole-pixel re-anchoring 10.59 found at the dagger's walk speed and called a reposition: at run speed the pixel byte lags the subpixel accumulator, and the reading comes back a whole pixel off until the carry lands. 10.58 called the residue a clamp with a memory; here it is a picture that does not change.

**The frame buffer was not what the core asked for.** Building the video required reading the pictures, and the first exported frame was a half-width magenta smear. The cause is in the capture path rather than the level: the core answers `RETRO_ENVIRONMENT_SET_PIXEL_FORMAT` with 2 (RGB888, four bytes per pixel) and then hands back 1 (RGB565, two) - pitch 2048 for a 256-px row, the second half of every row zero, and the lane a 0x00RRGGBB word keeps at zero carrying picture data. `refine_pixel_format` now checks the request against those two facts a genuine buffer cannot violate, and `tests/test_pixel_perception.py` holds both the positive control (a 16-bit row under a 32-bit request is demoted) and the negative one (a real RGB888 row is not).

**What this changes, and what it does not.** It does not move any telemetry result: every state, residue, camera and mode number in this repository comes from WRAM, and WRAM was never decoded through the pixel path. What it does touch is any claim this file has made about what a frame *shows*. The two such claims are the pixel probe of 10.31 and the picture check of 10.36.3, both run on frames decoded as 32-bit, so both were measured again with the fixed decoder on the same forced-mode savestate they were made on: driven for 150 frames the state moves 115.0000 px while 0 of 4 frame pairs translate at all, and the picture is the file-select menu - ![The forced-mode session draws the menu while the physics runs](../../results/figures/dashboard_forced_mode.png). Both sections' readings were therefore right and for the right reason, and the shift measurement survives the old decode anyway: the same squeeze applied to every frame cannot invent a horizontal translation. What the fix does change is this repository's ability to look at anything it records, which is what the dashboard above is for.
